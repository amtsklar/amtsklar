## Skript (Sprechtext)


### Intro

Plug in your phone at 3 a.m. Then plug it into the same outlet at 3 p.m. Same wall, same wires.

Depending on where you live, the power plants on the other end can be completely different. At night, maybe a wind farm on the plains. In the afternoon, solar panels that were dark at 3 a.m.

Stick with me, and you'll see why, in sunny states, the toughest hour for the grid often isn't the hottest one. And why, some days, power plants pay to give their electricity away.

Because the grid isn't one machine. It's a different machine every hour. So let's follow one day, hour by hour.


### 3 a.m.: The night shift

Let's start in the dark. According to the Energy Information Administration, U.S. electricity use typically bottoms out around five in the morning. Most people are asleep, and most offices are closed.  
  _Grafik: line chart: typical U.S. hourly electricity demand over 24 hours, low point near 5 a.m._

But it never drops to zero. Refrigerators and other devices that run all the time keep going. The EIA calls that steady floor of demand baseload.

The classic baseload plant is nuclear. It's cheap to run once it's built, so operators try to keep it at full power around the clock.

In 2025, the average U.S. nuclear plant ran at 91 percent of its maximum possible output, higher than any other kind of power plant, according to the EIA. Nuclear supplied about 18 percent of the country's utility-scale electricity that year.  
  _Grafik: flat line chart: nuclear output across 24 hours vs. curved demand line_

Now add the wind. In the middle of the country, the EIA says land-based wind is usually strongest overnight. So wind records there tend to fall in the early morning hours.

Take the Southwest Power Pool, the grid operator for a big stretch of the Great Plains. We ran its 2025 numbers from the EIA's hourly grid data. Averaged over the year, wind covered about 46 percent of demand at 2 a.m., but only about 31 percent at noon. And for five straight hours before dawn on December 28, 2025, it covered about 80 percent.  
  _Grafik: line chart: SPP 2025 average wind share of demand by hour of day (Central time), ~46% at 2 a.m. vs ~31% at noon; callout 'Dec 28, 2025: ~80% for 5 hours before dawn'; source note 'EIA Hourly Electric Grid Monitor (EIA-930), our calculation'_

In 2023, wind made up 36 percent of that grid's total generation. Some nights there's more wind than anyone needs, so operators turn turbines down in the early morning to keep supply matched to demand.


### 3 p.m.: The solar shift

Twelve hours later, the machine looks completely different. The sun is high, air conditioners are running, and in a growing number of places, solar is doing the heavy lifting.

Texas is a good example. In the summer of 2025, solar plants on the main Texas grid averaged 24 gigawatts between noon and one p.m. Two summers earlier, it was 12. That's double in two years.  
  _Grafik: bar chart: ERCOT average solar output at noon, summer 2023 (12 GW) vs. summer 2025 (24 GW)_

And natural gas made room for it. Gas supplied half of the Texas grid's midday power in the summer of 2023, and 37 percent in the summer of 2025.

California has gone even further. According to the EIA, solar now supplies almost half of the California grid operator's demand between eight in the morning and four in the afternoon.

And in the first five months of 2026, utility-scale solar generated more electricity than natural gas on 82 percent of days on that grid, up from 21 percent in 2024 and 2025.

So at 3 a.m. on the Great Plains you might be running largely on wind, and at 3 p.m. in California, close to half of it might be sunshine. Same country, same kind of outlet, a different machine.


### The duck curve

Now here's the twist I promised. You'd think the toughest moment for the grid is the hottest afternoon, but often it isn't. In places with lots of solar, it's the stretch around sunset.

To see why, grid operators look at something called net load. That's total demand minus whatever wind and solar are producing. It's the part every other power plant has to cover.  
  _Grafik: diagram: total demand minus wind and solar equals net load, stacked area illustration_

Plot net load over a typical day in California and it sags deep in the middle, then shoots up steeply in the evening. It looks like the outline of a duck, so it's called the duck curve. And the EIA says the belly keeps getting deeper as solar grows.  
  _Grafik: line chart: CAISO net load on a typical spring day, duck-shaped curve with belly at midday and steep neck at sunset_

The belly has its own problem: in spring, when it's mild and demand is low, there can be more solar than the grid can use. In 2024, California's grid operator curtailed 3.4 million megawatt-hours of wind and solar, 29 percent more than in 2023. Solar was 93 percent of it.

Then comes the neck. As solar fades around sunset, people get home, cook dinner and turn on lights and air conditioners. Everything that isn't the sun has to ramp up fast.

So keep that steep neck in mind. Because the most interesting part of this story is what's being built to fill it, and what it does to the price of electricity.


### Batteries: moving noon to night

One fast-growing answer is a battery. Charge it with cheap midday solar, then let it run in the evening. The EIA puts it neatly: storing midday solar flattens the duck's curve, and sending it out in the evening shortens the duck's neck.

The scale has changed fast. California's grid operator reported that in 2025, batteries in its market delivered about 9,100 megawatts on average in the hour ending at 8 p.m. In 2024, that figure was about 5,700.  
  _Grafik: line chart: average battery charging (midday, negative) and discharging (evening, positive) by hour, CAISO 2024 vs. 2025_

Texas is following the same pattern. In the summer of 2025, batteries on its main grid supplied an average of 4 gigawatts in the 8 p.m. hour, right as the solar output was fading.

Batteries don't make energy, though. They shift it, and they lose some along the way. In 2019, the EIA found the U.S. battery fleet gave back about 82 percent of the electricity put into it.

And operators run them largely on price. In 2024, 41 percent of U.S. utility-scale battery capacity was used mainly for arbitrage. That's a fancy word for charging when power is cheap and selling it back when it's expensive.


### Why prices swing

Which brings us to price. On the wholesale market, where power is bought and sold in bulk, electricity can cost wildly different amounts from one hour to the next.

Berkeley Lab found that the average wholesale price across the major U.S. power markets in 2023 was 36 dollars per megawatt-hour. But that average hides huge swings. That year in Texas, hourly prices on some summer evenings topped 500 dollars. In California, on sunny spring days, they dropped below zero.  
  _Grafik: two heat maps (LBNL ReWEP 2023, regional average hourly price by day x hour): ERCOT with >$500 summer-evening hours in pink; CAISO with sub-zero sunny spring hours in light blue_

Below zero. That's the strange part I promised at the start. A negative price means sellers are paying buyers to take their power.

Why would anyone do that? The EIA explains that some plants can't easily or cheaply shut down for a short stretch. Nuclear operators try to run at full power, and big steam plants face costs every time they stop and restart.

And some wind farms earn a tax credit for every megawatt-hour they produce, so they may keep running even when the price dips below zero. For a short stretch, paying a little beats shutting down.

In California's day-ahead market, prices were negative about 7 percent of the time in 2025, according to the grid operator's own market monitor. And Berkeley Lab found that in 2023, California's negative prices showed up almost entirely in sunny hours of late spring and early summer.  
  _Grafik: bar chart: share of negative-price intervals in CAISO day-ahead market, 2024 (8.8%) vs. 2025 (7.1%)_

On the other end are the expensive hours, when the grid calls on peakers. These are often gas turbines that run only occasionally. They cost more to run, but they respond quickly, which is exactly what that evening ramp needs.

Someone has to coordinate all of this. That job belongs to balancing authorities, dozens of them across the country, keeping supply and demand matched in real time.

If supply and demand drift too far apart, the EIA warns, the result can be local or even wide-area blackouts. In the big regional markets, prices help with that job. The EIA notes they tend to fall when solar output is high and rise as it fades.

And you can watch it yourself. The EIA's free Hourly Electric Grid Monitor shows demand and the mix of sources by region, hour by hour.


### Your plug, your timing

So what does this mean at your house? Most of us never see wholesale prices. But your timing still matters.

Most electric vehicle drivers charge overnight at home, according to the Department of Energy. And some utilities offer time-of-use rates, where electricity costs less in certain hours and more in others.

That's possible because most homes now have smart meters that record use at least hourly. In 2022, about 73 percent of U.S. residential electricity meters were smart meters, the EIA reports.

And here's where the old advice breaks down. On California's wholesale market, some of the cheapest hours are now in the middle of the day, not the middle of the night. So read your own utility's rate plan instead of guessing.

Running the dishwasher or charging the car in a cheap hour isn't just about your bill. It's you helping the grid fill the duck's belly instead of its neck.


### Recap

Quick recap. At 3 a.m., demand is near its low, nuclear runs flat out, and on the Great Plains, wind is often at its strongest.

At 3 p.m., solar takes over more and more of the load, sometimes so much that prices go negative. Then at sunset comes the steep neck of the duck, and batteries and gas plants race to cover it.

The grid isn't one machine. It's a different machine every hour. And now you know how to read it.
